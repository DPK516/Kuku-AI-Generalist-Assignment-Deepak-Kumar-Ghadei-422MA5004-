The problem, who has it, and how I know it's real

Who: the story/continuity editor who signs off a batch of AI-microdrama scripts before they go to AI video and voice generation. One step: the pre-render check. One checkable output: a list of continuity issues, each with episode and timestamp evidence, plus a canonical story bible for the pipeline.

Evidence 1, viewers. Animatic Media's Microdrama audience report, Nov 2026 coded 2,630 public viewer posts from October 2026. Plot logic drew 116 complaints and 15 explicit quits, the most of any reason (endings: 196 and 12). The AI flaws viewers named most were look-alike faces (48) and voices, accents and mispronounced names (39). In September they were face drift between shots (69) and sudden voice changes (62). Dubbing problems: 11 complaints, all 11 ended in a quit.

Evidence 2, Kuku's own pack. I ran the tool on the 50 episodes of I Married a Broken Soldier. The Duke's estate is spelled six ways (Ashburton ×8, Ashbourne ×8, Ashburn ×6, Ashbury ×5, Ashburg ×4, Ashburgh ×4). The hero is "Alexander Ashburn" in Ep 42 and "Alexander Ashburton" in Ep 45. Clarissa becomes "Clarisse" in Ep 31. The sister speaks as LADY IN GOLD GOWN (Ep 1–3), then CLARISSA, then LADY CLARISSA. The hero has three labels, and the late mother's book has seven names (ledger, herbal ledger, remedy journal, little book…).

Why this breaks AI shows specifically: a generation pipeline takes the script literally. The speaker label picks the voice and the face reference, a spelling picks the pronunciation and subtitles, and a prop noun picks what gets drawn. Three labels for one hero means three chances of three voices. That is the same flaw viewers name most.

Why this over Problems 1 and 2

  • P2 is a good product, but it isn't Kuku's business.
  • P1 brings viewers in; this keeps them. Plot logic and name/voice drift are where viewers explicitly quit, so every acquired viewer who leaves at Ep 5 is wasted promo spend.
  • Cheapest fix point. A rename in the script costs seconds. The same error after rendering means re-generating shots and re-dubbing lines, across 50+ episodes, for every show, every week.
  • Honest counterpoint: endings are the #1 complaint and this tool does nothing for them. P1 has the clearer short-term revenue link. I built all three; if I had to pick one for Kuku to own, it would be this one.

How it works

Rules (code)Clusters near-identical proper nouns; real names only (capitalised mid-sentence, never used in lowercase). Groups speaker labels for one person, and knows LADY X / LORD X are a couple.
Who is "LADY IN GOLD GOWN"? (AI)A small, scoped question per unnamed label, showing only the episodes where it speaks.
Verify (code)The AI's quote must exist in the script. Two labels that speak in the same scene can't be one person; this rule blocked a wrong "Dowager Duchess = Catherine" merge.
Props + logic (AI)Groups prop names that are one object; a fact ledger per 10 episodes, then contradictions.
Bible (code)Canonical label → all aliases, places and objects. Built from verified clusters, not free text.

Design lesson: my first version asked the model for everything in one 150K-character reconcile call. It duplicated the rule findings and produced a story bible with wrong identities (it mapped "THE FORMER GROOM" to the hero). Splitting the work into small, verifiable questions fixed most of that.

Where it fails

  • A real quote isn't proof. The model justified "BLOND NOBLEMAN = Lord Harrington" with a quote that exists but doesn't prove it. The same-scene rule can't help because they never share a scene. The label may even be reused for two different characters (Ep 2/9 vs Ep 27–44). Next step: a second, independent "does this quote prove X is Y?" check, and flagging labels reused far apart.
  • Spelling clusters need a human: Ashworth Manor (Ep 12) vs Ashwood (Ep 35) may be two places. The tool says "check" rather than auto-fixing.
  • Script only. It can't see the rendered video, so a correct script can still render two faces. The story bible it outputs is what the casting and reference-image step should consume.
  • Logic contradictions are the weakest part with the free Lite model. It finds a few, and code marks any it can't back with two quotes from different beats as unverified. A stronger model is a one-line swap.

How I'd know it worked with a real team

  • Editor acceptance rate (the "Real issue / Not an issue" buttons): target ≥ 70%. Below that, editors stop reading.
  • Issues fixed before render per show, and re-render / re-dub minutes avoided (track how many shot re-generations were caused by continuity errors before and after).
  • Audience signal: share of comments mentioning names, voices or "different actress", and the episode-to-episode drop-off at episodes where a label or name drifted, compared on shows checked vs not checked.

Scale and cost

Rules run in 0.1s for 50 episodes at zero cost. The AI pass is 28 Lite calls (~200k tokens) per 50-episode show, ≈ $0.03 at Flash-Lite-class prices, or under $1 with a frontier model. It can run automatically whenever a batch of scripts lands, with the report waiting for the editor. The story bible persists per show, so later batches only check new episodes against it.

AI tools I used, and where they got it wrong

Claude Code (Opus) built the engine and UI with me; Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite runs the AI passes; I used Chrome to research viewer complaints. Where AI got it wrong: Gemini's one-shot story bible confidently mixed up identities, and later a label merge was justified by a real but irrelevant quote. Claude's first name-variant rule flagged ordinary words ("Brittle/British", "Throw/Thrown") as name drift until I added the proper-noun filters. Every AI claim in the report carries evidence that code has checked against the script.
